# Who keeps Ohio’s memory?

*A state archive can preserve millions of records. The people who make those records, describe them, and decide what can be shared shape the story too.*

Memory does not live in one building. In Ohio it sits in state collections, local libraries, tribal archives, neighborhood organizations, family albums, and recordings made while history is still unfolding. A useful visit begins with a simple distinction: an archive holds evidence; an institution or community tells us how that evidence was assembled and what it can mean. Neither one automatically represents every person connected to a place.

Ohio History Connection (OHC) is a statewide institution with several different roles. It operates the State Archives and State Historic Preservation Office, maintains museums and historic sites, and collects objects and records connected to Ohio. Its collections include objects, manuscripts, photographs, audiovisual records, and oral histories. The Ohio Memory portal also joins material from OHC with collections contributed by community institutions. A digital item may therefore be held by one partner, described by another, and displayed through a shared portal. Follow the collection credit and record page before citing it.

The source type affects what we can learn. A letter documents what one writer chose to put on paper. A government file can show how an institution classified people and events. An oral-history recording gives a named narrator the chance to speak in their own voice, but it is still shaped by the interview question, date, recording conditions, transcription, and permissions. A museum label is an interpretation built from evidence, not the artifact’s own voice. Read each record for what it can support, then notice what it leaves out.

Ohio Memory’s oral history portal explicitly describes oral history as a way to preserve individual experience and record communities as they change. Its featured collections include interviews with LGBTQ+ Ohioans, Black community histories, and other local voices. The Ohio History Connection’s GOHI initiative has gathered oral histories with people who shaped LGBTQ+ communities; transcripts and recordings identify interviewees and topics, including organizers and bar owners. Petie Brown, CJ Curtis, and Judy Long, for example, are listed together in the Summit Station oral-history project. Their testimony belongs to them. A reader should follow the transcript and collection description rather than turn a short excerpt into a claim about all LGBTQ+ Ohioans.

Community collections can also change who controls the frame. OHC’s community collections include the Gay Ohio History Initiative community collection, developed with community partners, and local-library collections focused on neighborhoods, schools, veterans, and businesses. These are not just convenient portals. They document that history is collected through relationships: people decide to contribute records, institutions preserve and describe them, and communities may help define the questions. If you want to share a family or organizational archive, ask the repository about its collecting policy, access restrictions, copyright, and whether it can preserve the original context.

Indigenous materials call for another careful distinction. Ohio’s archaeological and historical collections include material from Indigenous cultures, but objects held by a state institution are not a substitute for present-day tribal knowledge or authority. Ohio History Connection’s own American Indian policy recognizes the importance of tribal relationships in interpretation and collections care. The Eastern Shawnee Tribe of Oklahoma has a digital library partnership with OHC, described by the Tribe as a project to present its past and cultural heritage and to expand the collection as records are recovered. This is a primary route into materials selected and described by the Tribe. It should be credited as the Eastern Shawnee Tribe’s archive, not generalized as a view held by all Shawnee people or all Native nations.

Local repositories matter too. A public library or historical society may hold photographs, newspapers, school records, oral histories, maps, or organizational files that never entered a statewide collection. Ohio Memory’s catalog makes those contributions visible and gives the partner institution credit. Before traveling, check the repository’s current hours and reading-room rules; “digitized” can mean a catalog record or thumbnail exists, not that every page is online. For a research visit, contact the archive about advance requests, handling rules, copying, and permission to publish images.

Try a three-layer reading exercise with one Ohio place. First, find an institutional record: a map, directory, or official report. Next, find a community-held record: a local newspaper, organization archive, or collection built with residents. Then find a first-person account, whether it is a diary, oral history, or testimony. Ask who created each source, for whom, and under what conditions. Compare the language used to describe the same street, school, work site, or event. The differences are not noise to smooth away; they may be the most useful evidence.

This approach makes a visit more generous. Instead of asking an archive to confirm one tidy version of Ohio, ask what its records can show and what further voices are needed. Credit the narrator and collection partner. Keep dates and restrictions attached to a digital image. For living communities, look for present-day organizations and their own statements rather than assuming that a historical record speaks on their behalf. Preserving local memory is ongoing work, done by archivists and historians, but also by families, cultural organizations, tribal nations, libraries, and the people who trust them with their stories.

### Companion notes

- **A record is not the whole community:** OHC’s GOHI collection makes named interviewees, transcripts, and recordings discoverable. Cite the individual account and its context; do not use one person’s words as a group’s consensus. [GOHI oral histories](https://www.ohiohistory.org/gohi-oral-histories/)
- **Follow the holding institution:** Ohio Memory’s partner descriptions connect digital collections to the organizations that contributed them, including the Eastern Shawnee Tribe of Oklahoma. Start at the collection description before using an object image. [Ohio Memory collections](https://ohiomemory.ohiohistory.org/collections)
