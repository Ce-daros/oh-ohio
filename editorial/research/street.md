# Street dossier: Race Street, Cincinnati, across time

**Working title:** Race Street, with the market at its north end  
**Draft length:** approximately 790 words  
**Retrieved:** 2026-09-25  
**Editorial purpose:** Add a historically grounded street portrait to the existing Cincinnati market walk. The place is the Race Street side of Findlay Market and the Over-the-Rhine blocks around it; historical material is distinguished from present-day route guidance.

## Publishable draft

### Find the street before the market

Race Street did not begin as a backdrop for brunch photographs. It was laid out as part of a land-development story north of Cincinnati’s old boundary. The Findlay Market history says James Findlay and Jepthah Garrard recorded the Northern Liberties plat in 1833. It established Race, Elm, Green, and Findlay streets, along with a market area used by local farmers. When the Northern Liberties were annexed in 1849, the street grid became part of the expanding city.

Use today’s Race and Elder streets as a small map anchor. Findlay Market gives its public address as 1801 Race Street, and its guide shows Race bounding the market’s west side. For a reproducible location pin near the market, the Corporation for Findlay Market lists 132 W Elder Street; the U.S. Census Bureau’s current address geocoder returns approximately **39.11544, −84.51930** for that address. That coordinate marks the organization’s address on the block, not the center of the market house. Keep the distinction if you draw a map.

### A market block in the nineteenth century

The market story came in stages. The iron-framed market house was erected in 1852, but contractor disputes and difficulties with the new construction delayed its opening until 1855. Cincinnati’s market system then had many locations: the market’s history records nine public markets at the start of the Civil War. A market was part of city provisioning, with butchers, fish sellers, farmers, and produce vendors selling perishables to people who lived nearby. Picture the work arranged across a block and its surrounding streets, rather than imagining today’s indoor counters already in place.

Over-the-Rhine was also a residential and working neighborhood shaped by migration and commerce. The market’s historical account describes German churches, cultural groups, newspapers, and breweries; by 1860 the neighborhood’s brewing industry included 36 breweries. It reports that most of the 950 structures in the Over-the-Rhine North district were built from 1860 to 1900. Those claims come from the market’s history and its cited local district report; they describe broad neighborhood conditions, not the experience of every household.

In 1902, a central masonry tower was added to the market. Public-health concerns about the open-sided structure, alongside urban pollution, led to enclosure and the addition of plumbing and refrigeration. One useful map activity is to compare an 1887 or 1904 Sanborn sheet with the present street plan. Sanborn maps document building footprints, uses, materials, and addresses for insurance purposes. They can show a built environment, but they do not tell us who felt welcome there, what a customer bought, or exactly what a busy street sounded like.

### The block changes with the city

Public markets lost some of their centrality in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries as city populations shifted, hilltop business districts grew, and refrigeration and automobiles changed shopping habits. The Findlay Market history says the market endured with the dense neighborhood around it. Its account records a major population decline in Over-the-Rhine during the 1960s and an increase in Black residents after West End residents were displaced by highway construction and urban renewal. These are consequential changes, not an atmospheric detail. Read the market’s account beside census records, local histories, and residents’ own historical work before turning them into a simple decline-and-revival story.

The National Register recognizes Over-the-Rhine as significant for architecture and for areas including industry, commerce, religion, social history, and performing arts. The district record places the historic area across several named boundaries. It helps explain why Race Street can be read as part of a larger place, but a designation does not capture all the people and contested choices that shaped the neighborhood.

### Walk it in the present tense

Today, Race is still a working route around a working market. The city’s streetcar connects downtown and Over-the-Rhine, with a stop named Findlay Market – Race. The city lists core service every day, with weekday, Saturday, and Sunday hours that differ; check the current schedule and fare page before travelling. Findlay Market currently lists itself open Tuesday through Sunday, with separate hours for the weekend farmers market. Shops and vendors may keep individual schedules, so a market visit is better planned around the current calendar than an old guidebook.

If you are there, compare a historic map with the street you can see. Notice which street lines remain, where the market house occupies a block, and how the streetcar station now meets the market entrance. A map can explain the sequence of plat, public market, urban neighborhood, and transit connection. What it cannot do is give you an eyewitness memory of any period. For that, we would need a diary, a recorded oral history, a photograph with a reliable caption, or a person willing to tell their own story.
