# Materials dossier: the life of an Ohio-made object

**Working title:** From brass blank to familiar signal  
**Draft length:** approximately 760 words  
**Retrieved:** 2026-09-25  
**Editorial purpose:** Expand the existing journal pieces on the Columbus whistle and Rookwood pottery, while pointing readers toward the existing Toledo glass and Akron rubber stories. The draft below stays with two objects whose makers publish process accounts; it does not imply that a visitor has watched the work or spoken with a craftsperson.

## Publishable draft

### A whistle begins flat

Take a metal whistle from a desk drawer and turn it over. Its sound seems to come from one tiny opening, but the object began as several carefully shaped pieces. American Whistle Corporation, which says it has made metal whistles in Columbus since 1956, describes a line that starts with brass sheet or coil. Presses cut blanks; forming tools make the body and mouthpiece; workers join the pieces around the air passage and place the small pea inside the chamber. The company describes finished whistles as hand-tested for sound.

That sequence gives us a useful way to read the object. Start at the mouthpiece and trace the route air would take. Look for the chamber, the opening, the seam, and the ring. Each feature belongs to the sound-making path or to the practical job of carrying and using the whistle. The company’s explanation of the pea whistle says the moving sphere interrupts the airflow in the chamber, producing the familiar trill. The pea is not decoration. It is a moving part whose room to travel matters.

The first close look is almost a miniature manufacturing diagram: sheet, cut, bend, join, test. The company says it still uses original machinery and produces more than a million whistles a year. Those are the maker’s own figures, useful for understanding how it presents its current operation, but they are not an independent production audit. Its current tour page advertises a guided factory visit; because dates, access, and arrangements can change, check the booking page before planning around it.

### Clay asks for time

In Cincinnati, another familiar object follows a slower path. Rookwood Pottery dates its beginnings to 1880, when Maria Longworth Nichols Storer established a pottery in a former schoolhouse and potters worked with Ohio River clay. Rookwood’s current account says each piece passes through at least thirteen sets of hands. The phrase is a strong doorway into the work, though the company does not publish a role-by-role ledger for every object. It does say that today it makes pottery, architectural tile, and custom work in Cincinnati.

Clay changes as it is shaped, dried, decorated, glazed, and fired. Each stage narrows the choices available at the next one. A curve that looks effortless on a finished vase has to survive those changes; a glaze is both a surface and the result of a process. Rookwood’s own description stresses patience and allows that small variations are part of handmade work. That gives a reader something specific to notice: not simply “craft,” but where a form is regular, where a glaze gathers, and where a maker has left a mark.

Storer’s stated design principle was, “Designs shall be original, individuality shall be the goal.” The brief line comes from Rookwood’s official history. It points to a tension still worth seeing in any workshop: each vessel is a usable thing, but it can also carry an artist’s distinct choices. The company’s history reports a first kiln in 1880 and early recognition at the 1889 Paris Exposition. Museum collections add an independent record of particular objects and dates; compare one cataloged historic piece with a current Rookwood object, and keep the two institutions’ descriptions distinct.

### Follow the material across the state

Now the two objects can open onto the journal’s existing Ohio material stories. A whistle makes airflow visible through sound; glass makes light and heat part of what we see; rubber turns flexibility and wear into a question about a tire’s contact with the road. Toledo’s studio-glass story adds a different kind of making history: the museum dates workshops in 1962 as a turning point for American studio glass. Akron’s rubber story connects everyday products to industrial research and labor. These are related paths, not one continuous Ohio factory story.

Try one small comparison. Put a whistle, a glazed cup, a glass vessel, and a tire tread in your mind’s eye. Ask what each material has to withstand, what process changes its shape, and what the finished object lets someone do. Then ask whose work the object makes easy to overlook. The answer may be a tool operator, a kiln worker, a designer, or a tester. The available sources establish manufacturing histories and published process descriptions; they do not let us speak for the people doing those jobs today.

If you are in Cincinnati, Rookwood lists its working facility and showroom at 1920 Race Street and currently offers scheduled factory tours. The maker states that tours enter a live factory, require closed-toe shoes, and cover a large area; it asks visitors with mobility questions to contact the company in advance. Confirm current availability and access arrangements directly. In Columbus, use American Whistle’s current visit page for tour details. The objects are worth a close look even if a factory visit does not fit your plans.
