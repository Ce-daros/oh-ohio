# Sounds dossier: Ohio, heard at work

**Working title:** A canal song, a city voice, a room full of players  
**Draft length:** approximately 780 words  
**Retrieved:** 2026-09-25  
**Editorial purpose:** Make sound a way to meet Ohio’s work and language without inventing a field recording or claiming a single statewide accent. Link readers to real institutional audio; do not copy recordings into the site absent rights clearance.

## Publishable draft

### A song carried along the canal

Start with a song that had a job to do. In the 1930s, folklorists John A. Lomax and Alan and Elizabeth Lomax recorded Captain Pearl R. Nye in Akron. Nye had been born and raised on a canal boat, and the Library of Congress collection presents his songs as evidence of life along the Ohio and Erie Canal. Its online collection describes 22 audio discs with 75 songs, alongside manuscripts, correspondence, and photographs. The sound is not a general “Ohio folk” soundtrack. It belongs to a particular performer, a particular history, and a network of people who worked and lived on the canal.

Open one of the Library’s linked recordings and listen for what the catalog lets you know: the performer, the disc and side, the song title, and the collection around it. Then listen again for what a metadata record cannot settle on its own. Who first taught Nye a song? How did the version change as it moved from person to person? A recording preserves a performance at one moment; it does not freeze the whole tradition. The collection’s handwritten song texts and photographs can help put the sound beside a person and a place.

That distinction matters because archives have a history of selection. Lomax’s equipment, travel, and collecting decisions shaped what was captured and how later listeners encounter it. The Library’s catalog identifies these as performances by Nye recorded in Akron, rather than pretending the archive is a transparent window onto everyone who sang on the canal. We can value the recording and still ask what did not make it onto the disc.

### A city has more than one voice

Ohio speech also resists a single signature sound. The Buckeye Speech Corpus, maintained by researchers at The Ohio State University, holds conversations recorded with 40 Columbus speakers and an interviewer. Its project page says the speech is transcribed and phonetically labeled, with timing aligned to the audio. That makes it a research collection, not a set of impressionistic “accent samples.” A listener can hear people speaking freely while researchers can examine particular sound patterns in context.

Columbus is one city, and 40 speakers are a defined sample, not a map of the whole state. The collection cannot prove what every Ohioan sounds like. It does give us a way to replace vague claims about “the Ohio accent” with a better question: which speaker, in which setting, using which pronunciation, and compared with whom? The corpus is free for noncommercial use after registration, according to the project site. The corpus license applies to corpus use; it is not a blanket license for embedding recordings in a commercial or public-facing site. Link to the official registration page and follow its terms.

### Performance has a working life

Then there is the sound people make together in a room. Cincinnati Music Hall opened in 1878 as both a music center and a place for industrial exhibitions. Today Cincinnati Arts lists the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra, Cincinnati Pops, Cincinnati Ballet, Cincinnati Opera, and May Festival among the organizations based there. A concert is the part an audience hears, but rehearsals, scheduling, contracts, instrument preparation, stage work, and the musicians’ ability to earn a living all sit behind that sound.

In Cincinnati, that labor has a long local history. AFM Local 1 says the Cincinnati Musicians’ Protective Union was incorporated in 1881 and became a local of the American Federation of Musicians in 1897. Its constitution describes personal contracts and a master agreement for Cincinnati Symphony musicians, with provisions for rates, conditions, and touring. The organization’s account is a primary source for how the union describes itself and its rules; it is not a full history of every musician or every dispute. A 2024 announcement by the orchestra and musicians records a more recent five-year agreement. Put those sources beside Music Hall’s institutional history and the building becomes a workplace as well as an auditorium.

Try a listening sequence that moves between these scales. First, follow one Pearl Nye recording through its catalog entry and related documents. Next, compare one voice in the Buckeye Corpus with another, paying attention to words and context rather than trying to imitate a person. Finally, check a current Cincinnati performance listing and notice the venue, ensemble, and people credited. Each link gives a different kind of evidence: archived performance, recorded conversation, and live work scheduled for an audience.

There is no need to pretend those sounds are interchangeable. A canal song, a Columbus conversation, and a symphony concert have different makers, histories, and rights. For another Ohio sound, the National Park Service has posted a short Beaver Marsh scene from Cuyahoga Valley National Park. The NPS describes it as having no spoken narration, with insects, frogs, birds, and occasional muffled visitor voices in the background. The linked file is an NPS-hosted MP4 with its video as well as its soundtrack; NPS credits it to NPS / Josh Angelini and says NPS-credited multimedia without a copyright mark is public domain. Listen through the official page, or use the direct MP4 with that credit. Sound is made by people, under real conditions, and it carries more than melody or accent. Sometimes it carries a job, a route, a memory, or the terms under which a performance can happen.

**Companion notes:**

1. **One canal song, three records**

   Captain Pearl R. Nye’s songs reached the Library of Congress on recording discs, but the archive preserves more than sound. The collection page links recordings with song texts, correspondence, and photographs. Choose one disc-side entry, then keep those records open together: the audio documents one performance, the written text lets you follow its words, and the related papers can show how a collector identified or discussed it. The Library describes the collection as recordings made in Akron in 1937 of songs connected to life on the Ohio and Erie Canal. Do not assume every item has the same date, setting, or rights status. Link to the Library’s player and that item’s catalog record; verify the individual rights statement before rehosting a file.

   **Sources:** [LOC collection overview](https://www.loc.gov/collections/captain-pearl-r-nye-life-on-the-erie-and-ohio-canal/about-this-collection/); [LOC finding aid](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdcfindingaidpdfs/af012006/af012006.pdf).

2. **What is an accent sample?**

   The Buckeye Speech Corpus records conversations with 40 Columbus speakers and an interviewer. Researchers transcribed the speech, added phonetic labels, and aligned those labels with the audio. That makes the corpus a useful place to listen closely to actual speech, with words and timing available for study. It does not tell us what every Ohioan sounds like: the speakers are a defined Columbus sample, and conversation changes with topic, audience, and setting. The project offers the corpus free for noncommercial use after registration, under its own license. For a public article, link readers to the project’s registration and license pages instead of copying a clip unless the terms clearly permit that use.

   **Sources:** [Buckeye Speech Corpus](https://buckeyecorpus.osu.edu/); [corpus license](https://buckeyecorpus.osu.edu/License.pdf).
